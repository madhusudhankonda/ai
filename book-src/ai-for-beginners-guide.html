1. Welcome — what this guide is for

I'm Madhusudhan, and I run live AI workshops through ChocolateMinds. This guide goes with the AI for Beginners session — the same journey we take in the room, but something you can keep, re-read, and use at home.

This is not a textbook full of theory. It's a workbook. By the end, you should know what AI is, how to talk to it properly, and how to go from a rough idea to a working app — even if you've never written a line of code.

You need three things: curiosity, clear thinking, and willingness to try. That's it.

2. Why AI matters now

AI isn't new. Banks, Netflix, Google Maps, and shopping sites have used it for years — often without you noticing. What changed recently is access. Tools like ChatGPT, Claude, and Gemini put AI in your hands. You type normal English; the tool responds.

That shift is huge. Before, building with AI meant large technical teams. Now a student, parent, teacher, or small-business owner can use AI as a thinking partner — to explain, plan, draft, brainstorm, and prototype.

Ask & learn

"Explain photosynthesis like I'm 12." Get a clear answer in seconds.

Plan & organise

Revision timetables, trip plans, weekly to-do lists.

Create

Stories, posters, emails, quiz questions.

Build

Describe an app; tools like Lovable or Replit generate a first version.

The catch Casual use → casual results. Learning to prompt well, check outputs, and iterate — that's where real value comes from. This guide teaches that habit.

3. What AI actually is (no jargon)

In plain terms: AI is software that does tasks we usually associate with human intelligence — understanding language, recognising images, answering questions, spotting patterns, writing text, or making predictions.

In our workshops we focus on Generative AI: systems that create new content — text, images, code, summaries, lesson plans, or app layouts. That's why it feels magical. It isn't magic.

What AI is good atWhat to remember
Drafting, brainstorming, explainingFast first versions — you refine
Following detailed instructionsClear prompts beat vague ones
Generating code from descriptionsYou still test and decide
Sounding confidentIt can be wrong — always check important facts

Generative AI learns patterns from huge amounts of data and predicts what should come next. It doesn't "understand" the world the way you do. Think of it as a very fast assistant: you stay in charge; your judgement matters.

4. Traditional AI vs Generative AI

Traditional AI is usually built for one job: fraud detection at a bank, film recommendations on Netflix, traffic prediction in Maps. Useful — but narrow.

Generative AI is flexible. One tool can explain science, write an email, summarise an article, or help design a quiz app. That flexibility is exciting — and it means you need to guide it with good prompts.

Traditional AIGenerative AI
PurposeOne specific taskMany tasks via conversation
You interact byUsing an app (often invisible)Typing prompts in natural language
OutputScore, recommendation, alertText, images, code, plans
Example"Suspicious transaction flagged""Create a revision plan for my GCSEs"

A short history (why now feels different)

EraWhat happened
Decades agoAI in labs — chess, expert systems, research
2000s–2010sAI hidden in apps you already use (search, maps, recommendations)
2022 onwardChatGPT and similar tools — anyone can type and get answers
TodayApp builders let you describe software and get a working prototype

You don't need the history to build apps — but it helps to know: you're not "late." This wave is still early, and small builders are experimenting every day.

6. Prompting — clear beats clever

A prompt is your instruction to AI. No secret formula — just clear communication. The more specific you are about who it's for, what you want, and how you want it formatted, the better the result.

WeakStronger
"Make me an app.""Build a revision planner for GCSE students: subjects, exam dates, hours per day → weekly timetable. Clean, beginner-friendly UI."

The four-part prompt

PartQuestionExample
ContextWhat is this about?"For a 14-year-old revising maths"
RoleWho should AI act as?"Act as a friendly tutor"
TaskWhat should it do?"Explain fractions with two examples"
FormatHow should the answer look?"Then give 5 practice questions"
Try this now Open ChatGPT or Gemini and paste: "Act as a friendly AI tutor. Help a beginner understand fractions. Use simple examples, then give 5 practice questions with answers at the end."

Prompting is a conversation. Start → read the answer → improve your instruction → ask again. Don't expect perfection on the first try. Iteration is the skill.

10 prompts to try this week

Explain simply"Explain [topic] to me as if I'm 12. Use one everyday example."
Study help"Create 10 quiz questions on [topic]. Medium difficulty. Answers at the end."
Plan my week"I have exams in [subjects] on [dates]. I can study 2 hours/day. Suggest a 7-day plan."
Improve writing"Make this email clearer and shorter, but still polite: [paste text]"
Brainstorm"Give me 5 simple app ideas for students aged 13–16. Problem + one-line solution each."
Compare"What's the difference between ChatGPT and Gemini in plain English?"
Check my work"Here is my answer to [question]. Tell me what's right, what's wrong, and how to improve."
Creative"Write a 200-word story for a 7-year-old. Character: a curious robot. Theme: friendship."
Step by step"Break this task into 5 small steps I can do today: [task]"
App design"Act as a product designer. List the 3 main screens for a habit tracker for teens."

7. Turn an idea into a use case

"I want to build something with AI" is a start — not a plan. Sharpen it into a use case: who it's for, what problem it solves, and what success looks like.

Five questions

  1. Who is the user?
  2. What problem do they have?
  3. What input will they provide?
  4. What output should the app produce?
  5. What does success look like?
App ideaUserProblemSuccess
Revision plannerStudentDoesn't know how to split study timeClear weekly plan by subject
Birthday invite makerParent / teenNeeds a nice invite fastReady-to-share invite text or design
Quiz appLearnerWants to test knowledgeScore + explanations after 5 questions
Beginner trap Building too much on day one. Start small. One useful feature. Test it. Then add more. Small apps are easier to finish — and finishing builds confidence.

9. Build without code: the loop

In the workshop we don't type code by hand. We use AI-assisted builders. The loop is always the same:

Prompt
→
Build
→
Test
→
Improve

After the first version, ask yourself: Does it open? Are fields clear? Does the button work? Is the output useful? Would a friend understand it without you explaining?

Then prompt again: "Add placeholder text." "Show the plan in a weekly table." "Make it mobile-friendly." That's modern building.

Your first session at home (about 45 minutes)

  1. 10 min — Open a chat tool; try two prompts from Chapter 6.
  2. 5 min — Fill in the use case worksheet (Chapter 7).
  3. 20 min — Pick one project (Ch. 10, 11, or 12); paste the starter prompt in Lovable or Replit.
  4. 5 min — Send the link to one friend or family member.
  5. 5 min — Write one improvement prompt and apply it.
You don't need to read every line of code You do need to know what you want the app to do. Clear thinking → clear prompts → better apps.

10. Project 1 — Revision planner

Problem: Students often have multiple subjects and exam dates but no structured plan — so they revise randomly or panic near the exam.

Solution: A simple web app: enter subjects, dates, and available hours → get a weekly timetable.

Starter prompt (copy this)

Build a simple revision planner web app for beginners. The user enters: subjects, exam dates, difficulty per subject, and available study hours per day. The app generates a weekly revision timetable. Keep the interface clean, friendly and easy to use. Include a "Generate plan" button and a reset button.

After the first version — improve

  • "Show the plan in a table with days of the week."
  • "Add a short motivational message at the end."
  • "Add example placeholder text in each field."
  • "Make it suitable for GCSE and A-Level students."
Version one rule You're not building Google Calendar. You're building one useful thing that works. Ship v1, then improve.

Step-by-step in the workshop

  1. Open Lovable or Replit and start a new app project.
  2. Paste the starter prompt (above) and wait for the first build.
  3. Click through every field — does the plan make sense?
  4. Ask a friend: "Try this without me explaining."
  5. Pick two improvements from the list below and prompt again.

11. Project 2 — Story generator

This project shows AI isn't only for productivity — it's creative too. Same idea → prompt → app flow, different tone.

Starter prompt

Build a simple story generator app. The user enters: main character, story theme, age group, and moral of the story. The app generates a short story in a friendly tone. Add options for funny, adventurous, or emotional styles. Keep the design simple and colourful.

Teaching point

A story for a 5-year-old reads differently from one for a teenager. A funny story differs from an emotional one. Always tell AI the audience and tone — in apps and in everyday writing.

Improvement prompts

  • "Add a copy button for the story."
  • "Add 'Generate another story'."
  • "Make language simpler for young children."
  • "Show the moral in a separate box at the end."
📖 Story Generator — example layout

Why this project matters

Productivity apps feel "serious." Creative apps show that AI adapts tone — funny, gentle, dramatic. That skill transfers to emails, presentations, and social posts: always say who the audience is.

Starter prompt

Build a simple quiz app for beginners. The user enters a topic and chooses difficulty: easy, medium, or hard. The app generates 5 multiple-choice questions. After the user submits answers, show the score and explain correct answers in simple language. Keep the design clean and friendly.

Improvement prompts

14. Use AI safely

Three rules I give every group:

1. Don't blindly trust AI

It can be wrong while sounding confident (hallucination). Verify anything important — health, law, money, exams, public facts.

2. Protect private information

Don't paste passwords, bank details, medical records, or confidential school/work documents into random tools.

3. AI assists — you think

For students: use AI to explain, quiz, and check understanding — not to skip thinking. For everyone: AI gives speed; you give direction and responsibility.

For parents & teachers Talk about what kids paste into AI and whether school work is their own. The goal is learning with AI, not replacing learning.

16. The builder mindset

The biggest mistake I see: waiting until you "know enough" before building. You learn by building.

  • Start small — one feature, one screen, one useful output.
  • Break it, fix it, show someone, get feedback.
  • Don't compare your first app to professional software — unfair and unnecessary.
  • One app you built beats ten tutorials you only watched.
Start small
→
Prompt well
→
Test honestly
→
Improve

After your first app, the second is easier. Then the third. You move from "beginner" to "builder" by doing — not by collecting certificates.

Appendix B — Glossary

AISoftware that performs tasks we associate with human intelligence.
Generative AIAI that creates new content — text, images, code, etc.
PromptYour instruction to an AI tool — what you type in the chat or app builder.
LLMLarge Language Model — the engine behind tools like ChatGPT; trained on lots of text.
HallucinationWhen AI states something false confidently. Always verify important facts.
IterationImprove by trying again — better prompt, test, fix, repeat.
Use caseA clear description of who uses something, why, and what success looks like.
PrototypeFirst working version — good enough to test, not necessarily perfect.
Vibe codingBuilding software by describing what you want; AI generates much of the code.
App builderPlatform (e.g. Lovable, Replit) that turns prompts into working apps.

Appendix C — Notes for parents & teachers

This guide supports a practical workshop for young people. A few things worth knowing:

  • No prior coding is required. We use AI-assisted tools; the learning goal is thinking clearly and using AI responsibly.
  • Supervision: Primary-age children benefit from an adult nearby for sign-up and sharing links.
  • Privacy: Remind learners not to paste personal details (full names, addresses, passwords) into AI tools.
  • Schoolwork: AI can help explain and practise — not replace the student's own thinking. Check your school's policy.
  • After the session: Ask "What did you build? Who is it for?" rather than "Was it perfect?" Simple apps are a success.
